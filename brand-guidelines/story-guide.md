# TRPL Story Guide — Reference Summary

**Source:** *Story Guide* (Theodore Roosevelt Presidential Library, Case International, September 2022), 351 pages. The full document is in this folder as `TRPLStoryGuide_CaseInt_hires.pdf` and supersedes any earlier drafts. This summary captures the institutional framework, storytelling tenets, style guidance, and sensitive-issue protocols that govern all TRPL communications.

---

## What Is the Story Guide?

The Story Guide serves three simultaneous functions:

**Reference Guide.** It establishes the North Star: the core principles, mission, vision, and values of the TRPL, made clear to every member of the team.

**Onboarding Document.** Because the TRPL is intended to be a 100-plus-year institution, the Story Guide is a resource for generations of contributors — so that future team members can access the founding mission and regenerate it in ways consistent with its core tenets, no matter at what stage of development they join.

**Coherence Handbook.** It ensures consistency across time and across teams. As a touchpoint common to anyone who works on the TRPL — employees, volunteers, contractors, consultants — the Story Guide keeps storytelling at the center of everything.

---

## Three Pillars

All TRPL storytelling flows from three foundational principles drawn from Roosevelt's life:

- **Leadership**
- **Citizenship**
- **Conservation**

The challenge is to curate an institution that, by exploring Roosevelt's legacy in these areas, influences participants on a personal level and invites them to contemplate what it means to be "Rooseveltian" in today's world.

## Project Goals and the Ecosystem

The pillars, mission, vision, and values align around a single goal: using Roosevelt's life and legacy to inspire the next generation of leaders, citizens, and conservationists to get in the arena and make the world a healthier, safer, more equitable, and sustainable place.

To accomplish this, the TRPL has developed an interconnected array of activities, programs, partnerships, and content — what the Story Guide calls "the Ecosystem." Every element of the Ecosystem — architecture, interpretive design, distributed story plan, onsite and global programming, partnerships, digital archive, content and media strategy, marketing and PR, and capital campaign — works in concert around a common purpose.

### Concentric Rings Model

The impact of the TRPL starts at its center (the Library) and radiates outward in concentric rings:

1. **TRPL & Local Neighborhood** — The Library and its surrounding distributed sites in Medora
2. **The North Region** — The "T.R. Triangle" linking Medora, Yellowstone National Park, and Mount Rushmore
3. **America** — Because T.R. was an American president, content and impact extend nationally
4. **The World** — The TRPL intends to be a global institution

## Participants, Not Visitors

At the TRPL, guests are the hero and have an active role to play. The institution uses the term **"participants"** instead of "visitors" or "audience" because those terms imply a passive role.

**Participant types include:** National Park visitors, conservationists and nature enthusiasts, Medora Musical visitors, T.R. enthusiasts and scholars, hikers/campers, indigenous culture scholars, wellness retreaters, American West road-trippers, cowboy enthusiasts, Medora and regional community groups, youth school groups, university groups, mountain bikers, horseback riding groups, critical historians, scholars and fellows, architecture buffs, veterans, and active/retired military.

## Distributed Storytelling

Stories created inside the Library must also play out beyond its walls. The Library should draw people in to push them back out, ready to "Dare Greatly, Think Boldly, Care Deeply, and Live Passionately."

The Library is the *beginning* of the adventure — with many other chapters to be experienced and discovered outside its walls, always with the participant at the center. Stories and experiences will be distributed across Medora, the national park, and the broader region. Roosevelt was called to heal, explore, and experience adventure in the Badlands; so will participants at the Library.

## Why T.R. Now?

> "It is of little use for us to pay lip-loyalty to the mighty men of the past unless we sincerely endeavor to apply to the problems of the present precisely the qualities which in other crises enabled the men of that day to meet those crises."
> — Theodore Roosevelt, "The New Nationalism"

Today, we face environmental, economic, political, social, and spiritual crises. Roosevelt's mantra — "Over, under, or through — but never around" — can help navigate them. Like us, Roosevelt faced enormous upheaval in his own time. In such eras of adversity, we must be brave. We must get into the arena.

The TRPL is also a place for personal healing — and a place to mend the fabric of the nation. Roosevelt had a unique ability to connect with people from many different backgrounds. Sometimes that made him a man of contradictions who defied categorization. Ultimately, it makes him a convening force.

## 10 Guiding Tenets

These foundational storytelling tenets guide the selection and creation of stories across the entire TRPL ecosystem. Not all tenets apply to every story, but all should always be considered.

### Tenet 1: Inspire Action In the Arena

The TRPL's fundamental goal is to inspire participants to action and fearless participation "in the arena" — specifically in the areas of leadership, citizenship, and conservation. Stories might equip participants to learn, to care, or to think. But the overall experience will also inspire them to *act.* Participants leave with a broader sense of their own abilities, better prepared to take action.

### Tenet 2: The Participant Is the Hero

At the TRPL, the participant is the hero and Theodore Roosevelt is the guide. Whether scripting an audio tour, building a website, mapping a guided hike, building a partnership with a local organization, or designing the museum itself, the choices of the institution should always place the participant at the center of the story. (Think: the classic hero's journey. The hero embarks on a quest, is encouraged by a guide, overcomes challenges, and emerges transformed — and so does the world.)

### Tenet 3: Stories Come to Life

"Do, don't tell." Participants are not passive audience members. The TRPL engages them in a multisensory adventure — feeling, seeing, touching, smelling, and tasting Roosevelt's world. Kids of all ages are activated by unpredictable, interactive experiences, with a variety of potential adventure pathways that encourage repeat visitation.

### Tenet 4: Nature Is Fundamental

The Library draws participants inside to encourage them to go outside. The landscape around the Library is a natural resource of experiences: action and adventure, learning and fieldwork, healing and rejuvenation.

The outdoors is where transformation — physical, mental, and spiritual — occurs. In his moments of growth, grief, and transformation, Roosevelt sought out nature. To the TRPL, nature is fundamentally democratic: the American outdoors is meant to be equally enjoyed by all. Programming and learning should support access for everyone, regardless of economic status, physical ability, or experience level.

### Tenet 5: Engage with Head, Hands, and Heart

Roosevelt was a man of action, a prolific reader and author of more than forty books, and a powerful empath who cared deeply about family and friendships, loss and love. TRPL stories unlock all three facets — "hands" (adventure and action), "head" (intellectual curiosity and learning), and "heart" (empathy, connection, and emotional resonance).

### Tenet 6: The People's Library

Often, a presidential library is the home of a single story about one person. The TRPL is home to many stories — those of an entire nation. With Roosevelt as guide, the TRPL unlocks the stories of participants and helps them become the authors of their own adventure. The TRPL gathers and preserves participants' stories of action, adventure, leadership, and citizenship. It is an archive of American character, not just one man's legacy.

### Tenet 7: Lessons Are for Today and Tomorrow

Roosevelt lived in the moment and planned for the future. Whether inventing the presidential primary system, conserving America's landscape, promoting women's suffrage, working toward national healthcare, reforming campaign financing, or launching the first phases of a social safety net — the subjects he tackled are of the utmost importance today. The TRPL highlights lessons from the past, their relevance to the present, and their implications for the future.

### Tenet 8: Worthy of Its Presidential Stature

"To whom much has been given, much shall be expected." The TRPL aims to break expectations, generate surprise and delight, and inspire real action. It will be a place where kids drag their parents in. That said, the Library must never veer into "amusement park" territory. It always maintains a sense of dignity and prestige, and all its actions and content remain worthy of its presidential mission.

### Tenet 9: Celebrate the Old but Embrace the New

Roosevelt memorized the classics in their original languages — French, German, Italian, and Latin. He was also the first president to fly in an airplane, drive a car, and dive in a submarine. He mastered traditional crafts and embraced modernity simultaneously. The TRPL celebrates both: excavating traditions like oral storytelling and embracing the latest technologies and media to bring those traditions to life for new audiences.

### Tenet 10: Humanize, Don't Lionize

Roosevelt was both a conservationist and a big-game hunter. He embraced hypermasculinity and wrote an advice column for *Ladies' Home Journal.* He counted Black Americans as friends but did not use his power to make significant progress toward bettering their position. The TRPL does not simplify these contradictions. It embraces them. It does not overlook flaws — it faces them head on. Roosevelt made errors. Sometimes he changed his mind. Sometimes he pandered for votes. He firmly resisted being put into a box. In short, he was human.

## Style Guide: Voice and Tone

> "The voice of Theodore Roosevelt guides and inspires the participant. The voice and tone of the TRPL should not replicate Roosevelt's words. It should replicate how he made people feel."

Roosevelt's core values — Dare Greatly, Think Boldly, Care Deeply, and Live Passionately — were apparent in every statement he made. He electrified people through empowerment and inspiration. All TRPL writing should generate that same energy in the reader.

**Voice attributes:** Action-oriented, Decisive, Open-minded, Heartfelt, Earnest.

**Voice is not:** Casual, Ironic, Clever for its own sake, Vague, Partisan, Academic.

Voice and tone should be consistent across all communication channels — whether intended for a student or a gala, a puzzle in the gift shop, or a capital campaign email. Executive, employee, participant-facing, and social channels should all reflect the same cohesive voice that embodies the institution's values.

### Practical Writing Tips

- Use precise language.
- Use the active voice.
- Use short sentences.
- Avoid jargon.
- Avoid negative language.
- Use inclusive language accessible to all education levels and communication styles.
- Prioritize clear messages that resonate across cultures.
- Gather research and expertise; fact-check everything.
- Use stories to make messages emotional and memorable.
- Embrace TRPL core values.
- Seek out rich, informative details.
- Be nonpartisan.
- Measure communications by whether they inspire action in the areas of Leadership, Citizenship, and Conservation — not by clicks, views, or opens.

### Quote Roosevelt Often

Roosevelt penned more than 150,000 letters, authored more than forty books, and delivered countless speeches, articles, and diary entries. There is an extraordinarily rich written record. When the right Roosevelt quote exists for a topic or tale, use it — and let him have the last word. *Always quote verbatim with source attribution. Never paraphrase and present it as a direct quote. If you cannot verify the exact wording, describe the idea he expressed and flag it for verification.*

### Describe, Then Do

When writing about the TRPL's experiences or content, suggest to-dos and action items. Plant seeds of long-term thinking. Encourage the institution's core pillars of Leadership, Citizenship, and Conservation.

## Style Guide: Look and Feel

> "The TRPL is a place with one foot grounded in the past and one marching toward the future. It is at once historical, contemporary, and timeless."

### Layer Time

Use the old to design the new. Whether designing a trail map or a website, find opportunities to bridge the past, present, and future. Employ the dynamism of layering — old photographs, modern fonts, building materials, juxtaposed timelines.

### Incorporate Location

Design should be responsive to its audience and environment. Build design from the landscape of the Badlands, the colors of the prairie, the materials of the region.

### Design Practical Tips

- Embrace juxtaposition.
- Layer information and materials.
- Mix media: pair old and new, black and white and color, analog and digital.
- Show — don't tell. Where possible, *do* — don't tell.
- Be inclusive and accessible.
- Be nonpartisan.
- Source locally.
- Find inspiration in nature.
- Choose practical over pretentious.
- Choose plainspoken over frilly.
- Convey energy and vibrancy.
- Ground choices in research.
- Embrace materiality and multisensory, full-bodied experiences.
- Be inspired by history but open to reinterpretation for the modern day.
- Use words and TR quotes while also being big and bold with color.
- Find inspiration in nature; borrow from the beauty of the Badlands.

## Media Strategy Principles

Roosevelt was a master storyteller and highly conscious of his own image. He planned his stories as he lived them — bringing cameramen to Cuba, walking New York City streets with journalist and photographer Jacob Riis, documenting the capture of three thieves with a camera to illustrate his account.

**Use many media.** Roosevelt shared stories through writing, oral storytelling, film, wax cylinder audio recordings, magazine illustrations, newspaper cartoons, and photography. TRPL content should similarly embrace diverse platforms and formats.

**Engage with existing content, then apply creativity.** Start with the rich volume of historical material. Engage in extensive research. Fact-check everything. Use original sources to inspire creativity — whether colorizing photographs, directing a web series, or designing interactive objects — to elevate facts into experiences.

**Use new technology.** Roosevelt embraced new technology throughout his life. Translate that love to today: think about how Roosevelt would have seized on a medium — how he would have used social media to take his bully pulpit directly to the people.

## Sensitive Issues

The TRPL confronts the problematic and sensitive parts of Roosevelt's legacy thoughtfully and directly, consulting with members of affected groups as well as historians and other experts to ensure appropriate responses and dialogue. (See Tenet 10: Humanize, Don't Lionize.)

Issues to address include but are not limited to:

**Social Darwinism and Eugenics.** Roosevelt openly supported eugenics and held white Europeans as the strongest and most evolved of the races. Content must confront this directly without softening or omitting it.

**Native Americans.** Roosevelt's views on and treatment of Native American peoples require direct, expert-informed treatment, with consultation from Native communities and scholars.

**Race and African Americans.** Roosevelt's contradictions here are stark: he was the first president to dine with an African American at the White House, a gesture that shifted the national conversation — while also writing that Black soldiers were only effective when led by white officers and refusing to give credit to African American Rough Rider soldiers. Both truths are told.

*Note: Content related to sensitive issues must be reviewed by subject-matter experts and relevant community representatives before publication. Do not draft or publish content on these topics without coordinating with institutional leadership.*

## The Badlands as Brand Asset

Roosevelt arrived in Medora in September 1883 to hunt buffalo. Dakota Territory was a rough region, barely connected to the Northern Pacific Railway lines — the perfect undeveloped place for Roosevelt to develop himself into the leader and citizen he would become.

He first arrived for sport and adventure. He returned the following year to heal his broken spirit — after the death of his mother and his wife on the same day. His mantra for recovery: *"Black care rarely sits behind a rider whose pace is fast enough."*

The Badlands are not incidental to Roosevelt's story. They are central to it. The TRPL is located where that transformation happened — adjacent to Theodore Roosevelt National Park, which bears his name, at the main entrance in Medora. The park honors the place where Roosevelt became an environmentalist and the cradle of American conservation.

All TRPL storytelling should lean into the Badlands landscape: the remoteness, the sense of adventure, the healing power of wide-open spaces.
